Metoprolol

Pronunciation: me TOE pro lol
Brand names: Kapspargo Sprinkle, Lopressor, Toprol-XL
Dosage form: oral tablet (immediate release), oral tablet (extended release), oral capsule (extended release), injection
Drug class: Cardioselective beta blockers

Medically reviewed by Melisa Puckey, BPharm. Last updated on Sep 10, 2026.

What is metoprolol?

Metoprolol is a beta-blocker medication used to treat high blood pressure, chest pain (angina), and heart failure, and to improve survival after a heart attack. It works mainly on the heart, so it is called a cardioselective beta-blocker.

Metoprolol works by blocking the effect of adrenaline and noradrenaline on beta-1 receptors in your heart. This slows your heart rate, reduces how hard your heart squeezes, and lowers your blood pressure. Because the heart is doing less work, it needs less oxygen, which is why metoprolol also helps with angina. Exactly how metoprolol works for high blood pressure, heart failure, and after a heart attack is not fully understood.

At higher blood levels, metoprolol also blocks beta-2 receptors in the airways and blood vessels, which is why it still needs care in people with breathing problems.

Metoprolol comes as two different salts, metoprolol tartrate and metoprolol succinate. They are not interchangeable dose for dose:

  • Metoprolol tartrate, an immediate-release tablet usually taken once or twice a day (brand name Lopressor)
  • Metoprolol succinate, an extended-release tablet or capsule taken once a day (brand names Toprol-XL and Kapspargo Sprinkle)

Metoprolol tartrate is also given as an injection into a vein in a hospital during a heart attack.

What is metoprolol used for?

Metoprolol is FDA-approved to treat high blood pressure, angina, heart failure, and heart attack, but the two forms are not approved for the same conditions.

Condition Metoprolol tartrate (immediate release) Metoprolol succinate (extended release)
High blood pressure (hypertension) in adults Yes Yes
High blood pressure in children 6 years and older No Yes
Angina (chest pain) Yes Yes
Heart failure No Yes
Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Yes No

High blood pressure (hypertension). Lowering blood pressure reduces the risk of fatal and non-fatal heart and blood vessel events, mainly strokes and heart attacks. Metoprolol can be used on its own or with other blood pressure medicines. Many people need more than one medicine to reach their blood pressure goal.

Angina. Metoprolol is used for long-term treatment of angina, to reduce how often attacks happen and to help you do more before chest pain starts. It is not used to stop an attack that has already begun.

Heart failure. Only the extended-release form is approved for heart failure. It is used to reduce the risk of dying from heart and blood vessel causes and to reduce hospital admissions for heart failure.

Heart attack (myocardial infarction). Metoprolol tartrate is used in people whose blood pressure and circulation are stable after a definite or suspected heart attack, to reduce the risk of dying from heart and blood vessel causes. In hospital, it may be started as an injection into a vein and then continued as tablets.

What is the difference between metoprolol succinate and metoprolol tartrate?

The two forms release the same medicine at different speeds, and they are approved for different conditions. Metoprolol tartrate is released all at once, so it acts sooner and wears off sooner. Metoprolol succinate is released slowly over the day, so one dose lasts about 24 hours. Only the extended-release form is approved for heart failure and for children, and only the immediate-release form is approved for use after a heart attack.

If you switch from immediate-release metoprolol to the extended-release form, the total amount you take in a day stays the same. Your prescriber makes this change, not you.

Metoprolol side effects

The most common side effects of metoprolol are tiredness, dizziness, low mood (depression), diarrhea, shortness of breath, a slow heartbeat (bradycardia), and itching or rash. Most side effects reported in studies were mild and did not last.

Get emergency medical help if you have signs of an allergic reaction to metoprolol: hives, difficulty breathing, or swelling of your face, lips, tongue, or throat.

Common side effects of metoprolol

In studies of people taking metoprolol for high blood pressure or angina:

  • Tiredness and dizziness occurred in about 10% of people
  • Low mood or depression was reported in about 5 out of 100 people
  • Diarrhea occurred in about 5% of people
  • Itching or rash occurred in about 5% of people
  • Shortness of breath and a slow heartbeat occurred in about 3% of people
  • Cold hands and feet, palpitations, swelling of the ankles, and low blood pressure were each reported in about 1% of people
  • Nausea, dry mouth, stomach pain, constipation, gas, and heartburn were each reported in about 1% of people

Headache, nightmares, trouble sleeping, confusion, and short-term memory loss have also been reported.

In the heart failure study of extended-release metoprolol, dizziness or vertigo was reported by 1.8% of people taking metoprolol compared with 1.0% taking placebo, and a slow heartbeat by 1.5% compared with 0.4%.

Other reported side effects

These have been reported since metoprolol came on the market. Their frequency is not known:

  • Hair thinning or hair loss, which reversed after stopping
  • Reduced sex drive and difficulty getting an erection
  • Dry eyes, blurred vision, and ringing in the ears
  • Sweating, sensitivity to sunlight, and worsening of psoriasis
  • Change in taste, joint and muscle pain, and fainting
  • Liver inflammation (hepatitis)
  • A severe drop in white blood cells (agranulocytosis), which can show up as fever, chills, or a sore throat

Serious side effects of metoprolol

Some of these, such as shortness of breath and a slow heartbeat, are also listed as common side effects above. They need urgent attention when they are severe, new, or getting worse.

Call your doctor at once if you have:

  • A very slow heartbeat, pauses in your heartbeat, or a feeling that you might faint. Sinus pause, heart block, and cardiac arrest have all been reported.
  • Signs that heart failure is getting worse, such as weight gain over a few days, more shortness of breath, or swelling of your feet or lower legs
  • Wheezing or trouble breathing
  • Chest pain that is worse or more frequent than usual, especially if you have recently stopped or missed doses
  • Cold, pale, or blue fingers and toes, or pain in your legs when you walk
  • Confusion, hallucinations, or memory problems
  • Yellowing of your skin or eyes, which can be a sign of liver inflammation

This is not a complete list of side effects and others may occur. Call your doctor for medical advice about side effects. You may report side effects to the FDA at 1-800-FDA-1088.

Metoprolol warnings

The most important warning with metoprolol is not to stop it suddenly. Stopping a beta blocker abruptly can bring on worse chest pain and, in some cases, a heart attack. If your treatment needs to stop, your prescriber will lower the dose gradually over 1 to 2 weeks and watch how you respond. This applies even if you only take metoprolol for blood pressure, because heart disease is common and is not always known about.

Talk to your care team before starting metoprolol if any of these apply to you.

  • Asthma or other breathing problems. Beta blockers can tighten the airways. Metoprolol is used in people with these conditions only when other treatments are not suitable, at the lowest possible dose, with a reliever inhaler on hand.
  • Heart failure. Heart failure can get worse while the dose is being increased. Tell your doctor about weight gain or increasing breathlessness.
  • A slow heart rate or a conduction problem in the heart. This includes first-degree heart block, sinus node problems, and Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome. Your heart rate should be monitored.
  • Diabetes or long gaps without food. Metoprolol can hide the early warning signs of low blood sugar, such as a fast heartbeat, and can make low blood sugar more severe or last longer. The risk is higher if you are fasting, not eating regularly, or vomiting.
  • An overactive thyroid. Metoprolol can mask signs of an overactive thyroid. Stopping it suddenly can trigger a thyroid crisis.
  • Circulation problems in your arms or legs. Beta blockers can make symptoms of poor circulation worse.
  • A history of severe allergic reactions. If you have anaphylaxis while taking a beta blocker, the usual dose of injectable epinephrine may not work as well.
  • Pheochromocytoma (a tumor of the adrenal gland). Metoprolol must only be started after an alpha blocker has been started, or blood pressure can rise sharply.
  • Liver disease. Metoprolol is broken down by the liver, so levels can build up. Treatment is usually started at a lower dose.

Surgery. Tell your surgeon and dentist that you take metoprolol. Long-term beta blocker treatment is not usually stopped before surgery. Starting a high dose of extended-release metoprolol shortly before non-cardiac surgery is avoided, because in one large trial this was linked to more slow heartbeats, low blood pressure, strokes, and deaths.

Who should not take metoprolol?

You should not take metoprolol if you have a very slow heart rate, heart block beyond the first degree, sick sinus syndrome without a pacemaker, cardiogenic shock, or heart failure that is not under control.

Metoprolol is not for you if you have:

  • Severe bradycardia (a very slow heart rate)
  • Second-degree or third-degree heart block, which is a fault in the heart's electrical signal
  • Sick sinus syndrome, unless you have a permanent pacemaker
  • Cardiogenic shock, when the heart cannot pump enough blood
  • Decompensated heart failure, meaning heart failure that is not stable
  • An allergy to metoprolol or to any of the other ingredients in your product

Immediate-release metoprolol tartrate tablets should also not be used if your top blood pressure reading is below 100 mmHg.

Metoprolol pregnancy and breastfeeding warnings (more detail)

Before taking metoprolol

Tell your doctor or pharmacist if you:

  • Take any other medicine for your heart or blood pressure, especially digoxin, clonidine, diltiazem, or verapamil
  • Take fluoxetine, paroxetine, quinidine, or propafenone, which raise metoprolol levels in your blood
  • Are pregnant, planning a pregnancy, or breastfeeding
  • Have liver problems
  • Have ever had a severe allergic reaction to anything, including foods and insect stings

Kidney problems do not usually change your dose. The amount of metoprolol in your body and how long it lasts are not meaningfully different in people with kidney failure.

How should I take metoprolol?

Take metoprolol at the same time each day, exactly as prescribed. Do not change your dose or stop taking it without talking to your prescriber first.

Immediate-release metoprolol tartrate tablets should be taken with a meal or immediately after a meal. For extended-release tablets and capsules, taking your dose with or just after a meal is preferred, but food does not have a significant effect on how much metoprolol your body absorbs.

  • Keep taking metoprolol even if you feel well. High blood pressure often causes no symptoms.
  • Extended-release tablets are scored and can be split in half if your prescriber tells you to. Swallow the whole or half tablet without crushing or chewing it.
  • Extended-release capsules (Kapspargo Sprinkle) should be swallowed whole. If you cannot swallow the capsule, you can open it and sprinkle the contents over a teaspoon of soft food such as applesauce, pudding, or yogurt. Swallow the mixture within 60 minutes and do not save any for later.
  • Tell your doctor if you are fasting or vomiting, because this raises your risk of low blood sugar.
  • Your blood pressure and heart rate will need checking regularly.
  • Store metoprolol at room temperature, between 68°F and 77°F (20°C to 25°C), away from moisture and heat.

Metoprolol injection is given into a vein by a healthcare professional in a coronary care unit or similar setting, where your blood pressure, heart rate, and heart tracing can be watched. You are then switched to metoprolol tablets.

Metoprolol dosage

The usual starting dose is 25 mg to 100 mg once a day for extended-release metoprolol, and 100 mg a day for immediate-release metoprolol. Your dose is then adjusted at intervals of a week or longer until your blood pressure or symptoms are controlled.

Metoprolol tartrate (immediate-release tablets) dosage for adults

Condition Starting dose Usual range
High blood pressure 100 mg a day, as one dose or split into two 100 mg to 450 mg a day. Doses above 450 mg a day have not been studied.
Angina 100 mg a day, split into two doses 100 mg to 400 mg a day. Doses above 400 mg a day have not been studied.
After a heart attack 50 mg every 6 hours for 48 hours, starting 15 minutes after the last dose given into a vein. If the full intravenous dose was not tolerated, 25 mg is used. Maintenance is 100 mg twice a day, continued for at least 3 months.

With immediate-release tablets, a once-daily dose may not keep blood pressure controlled for the full 24 hours, especially at 100 mg. Splitting the dose or taking a larger daily dose may be needed.

Metoprolol succinate (extended-release tablets and capsules) dosage for adults

Condition Starting dose Usual range
High blood pressure 25 mg to 100 mg once a day Adjust weekly or less often. Doses above 400 mg a day have not been studied.
Angina 100 mg once a day Increase weekly until symptoms are controlled or your heart rate slows noticeably. Doses above 400 mg a day have not been studied.
Heart failure 25 mg once a day for two weeks, or 12.5 mg once a day in more severe heart failure Double the dose every two weeks to the highest dose you tolerate, up to 200 mg once a day.

Kapspargo Sprinkle capsules are not suitable to start heart failure treatment if a dose below 25 mg is needed.

Metoprolol dosage for children

Extended-release metoprolol is the only form approved for children, and only for high blood pressure in children aged 6 years and older. The starting dose is 1 mg for each kilogram of body weight once a day, up to a maximum first dose of 50 mg. Doses above 2 mg/kg, or above 200 mg a day, have not been studied. Extended-release metoprolol has not been studied in children under 6 years.

Dose adjustments

  • Liver problems. Start at a lower dose than usual and increase slowly.
  • Kidney problems. No dose reduction is needed.
  • Older adults. A low starting dose is generally used, because liver, kidney, and heart function are more often reduced and other medicines are more often involved.
  • Switching between forms. When changing from immediate-release to extended-release metoprolol, the same total daily dose is used.
Detailed Metoprolol dosage information

What happens if I miss a dose of metoprolol?

Skip the missed dose and take your next dose at the usual time. Do not take two doses at once and do not double up to catch up.

What happens if I take too much metoprolol?

Seek emergency medical attention or call the Poison Help line at 1-800-222-1222.

Too much metoprolol can cause a dangerously slow heartbeat, very low blood pressure, and cardiogenic shock. It can also cause heart block, heart failure, wheezing, low oxygen levels, drowsiness or loss of consciousness, nausea, and vomiting. Overdose is treated in an intensive care setting. Dialysis does not remove metoprolol effectively.

What should I avoid while taking metoprolol?

  • Avoid driving and other tasks that need alertness until you know how metoprolol affects you. It can make you feel tired or less alert.
  • Get up slowly from sitting or lying down, because metoprolol can make you feel dizzy or lightheaded.
  • Avoid alcohol if you take Kapspargo Sprinkle capsules. Alcohol makes the capsule release its metoprolol much faster, which raises the risk of side effects.
  • Do not stop taking metoprolol on your own, even if you feel fine.

What other drugs affect metoprolol?

The interactions that matter most are medicines that also slow the heart and medicines that block the liver enzyme CYP2D6, which raises metoprolol levels in your blood.

  • Digoxin, clonidine, diltiazem, and verapamil. These all slow the heart's electrical signal and heart rate. Taking them with metoprolol raises the risk of a very slow heartbeat.
  • Clonidine, when stopping either medicine. If you take both, the metoprolol is stopped several days before clonidine is slowly withdrawn, because beta blockers can worsen the blood pressure rebound that follows clonidine withdrawal.
  • Quinidine, fluoxetine, paroxetine, and propafenone. These strong CYP2D6 blockers double the amount of metoprolol in your blood. Quinidine tripled levels of one form of metoprolol and doubled how long it lasted. Weaker CYP2D6 blockers are likely to raise levels too.
  • Reserpine and MAO inhibitors. These deplete adrenaline-type chemicals and can add to metoprolol's effect, causing low blood pressure or a marked slowing of the heart.
  • Epinephrine. If you need epinephrine for a severe allergic reaction, the usual dose may not work as well while you are on a beta blocker.
  • Other medicines that lower blood pressure or heart rate, including fingolimod, can add to metoprolol's effect.

Tell each of your healthcare providers about all medicines you take, including prescription and over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, and herbal products. Not all possible interactions are listed here.

Can you take metoprolol during pregnancy or while breastfeeding?

Metoprolol crosses the placenta and passes into breast milk in small amounts. Tell your doctor if you are pregnant, planning a pregnancy, or breastfeeding.

Studies published to date have not shown a link between metoprolol taken in pregnancy and major birth defects or miscarriage. Reports on growth restriction, early birth, and death around the time of birth have been inconsistent, and those studies have limitations that make them hard to interpret. Untreated high blood pressure and untreated heart failure in pregnancy carry their own serious risks for both mother and baby.

Babies born to mothers taking metoprolol may have low blood pressure, low blood sugar, a slow heartbeat, or slow breathing, so they are watched after birth.

The amount of metoprolol a breastfed baby receives is estimated at 0.05 mg to under 1 mg a day, which is about 0.5% to 2% of the mother's weight-adjusted dose. No side effects in breastfed babies have been identified. Babies are still monitored for a slow heartbeat and for signs such as unusual sleepiness.

Frequently asked questions about metoprolol

Is metoprolol a beta blocker?

Yes. Metoprolol is a beta blocker, and specifically a cardioselective beta blocker, which means it acts mainly on beta1 receptors in the heart rather than on receptors in the airways. That selectivity is relative, not absolute, and it weakens as the dose and blood levels rise.

How long does metoprolol take to work?

With immediate-release metoprolol tartrate, a measurable effect on heart rate starts within about 1 hour of taking a dose. Blood pressure is slower, and the full effect of a given dose usually takes about a week, which is why doses are increased at weekly or longer intervals.

Read more: How long does it take for metoprolol to work?

Should I take metoprolol in the morning or at night?

You can take metoprolol in the morning or at night, as long as it is at the same time each day. The labeling does not specify a time of day, so pick a time you can keep to. If you take it twice a day, space the doses about 12 hours apart, for example morning and evening.

If you take immediate-release metoprolol tartrate, that time should be with a meal or immediately after one. With extended-release tablets and capsules, a mealtime is preferred but not essential. If your prescriber has asked for a specific time, follow that.

How long does metoprolol stay in your system?

Metoprolol's half-life, the time it takes for half the drug to clear, is about 3 to 7 hours. Most people clear it within about a day, though it takes longer in people who break metoprolol down slowly because of their CYP2D6 enzyme.

Read more: How long does metoprolol stay in your system after you stop taking it?

Does metoprolol cause weight gain?

Weight gain is not listed as a side effect of metoprolol in the prescribing information. Rapid weight gain does matter for a different reason: it can be a sign that heart failure is getting worse. If you gain weight quickly, or with more swelling or breathlessness, contact your doctor rather than assuming it is the medicine.

Does metoprolol make you tired?

Tiredness is the most common side effect of metoprolol, reported in about 10% of people in studies. It happens because your heart is beating more slowly and with less force, and for many people it settles as the body adjusts. Tell your prescriber if it does not, and do not stop metoprolol on your own.

Read more: Does metoprolol make you sleepy?

Can metoprolol cause hair loss?

Hair loss has been reported with metoprolol since it came on the market, and it reversed after stopping. How often it happens is not known, because these reports come in voluntarily. Speak with your prescriber before stopping metoprolol if you notice hair thinning.

Read more: Does metoprolol cause hair loss?

Is metoprolol a blood thinner?

No. Metoprolol does not thin the blood or stop clots forming. It slows the heart and lowers blood pressure. People with heart disease are often prescribed a blood thinner as well, which is probably why the two get confused.

Can metoprolol cause erectile dysfunction?

Yes, it can. Beta blockers, including metoprolol, may cause erectile dysfunction and may reduce sperm movement, based on published research cited in the labeling. Reduced sex drive and difficulty getting an erection have also been reported since the medicine came on the market, though how often is not known. If this affects you, raise it with your prescriber, since your treatment can be reviewed.

Is metoprolol used for atrial fibrillation?

Yes, though this is an off-label use. Atrial fibrillation is not an FDA-approved use for metoprolol, but beta blockers are a first-choice option for controlling heart rate in people who have it. Off-label means prescribing a medicine outside its approved labeling, which is legal and common.

In atrial fibrillation the upper chambers of the heart beat in a fast, disorganized way. Metoprolol slows the electrical signal passing from the upper to the lower chambers, which brings the pulse down and can ease symptoms such as palpitations and breathlessness. Current guidelines recommend beta blockers or a type of calcium channel blocker as the first medicines to try for this, without preferring one class over the other for most people. Calcium channel blockers are avoided in people whose heart muscle is weakened, which is one reason metoprolol is often the choice there.

Neither salt is approved for this use, so the labeling does not say whether the immediate-release or extended-release form is better. That decision rests with your prescriber and depends on how quickly your heart rate needs controlling and how easy the dosing schedule is to keep to.

Metoprolol does not reduce the risk of stroke in atrial fibrillation. That is the job of anticoagulant medicines, and many people with atrial fibrillation take one alongside a medicine that controls their heart rate.

What happens if I accidentally take two doses of metoprolol?

Contact your doctor or pharmacist for advice, and call the Poison Help line at 1-800-222-1222 if you feel unwell. A double dose can cause a slow heartbeat, dizziness, and low blood pressure. Get emergency help if you faint, feel like you might pass out, or have trouble breathing.

How do you stop taking metoprolol?

Only under your prescriber's direction, and gradually. The dose is normally reduced over 1 to 2 weeks while you are monitored. Stopping suddenly can cause worse chest pain and, in some people, a heart attack. If chest pain worsens sharply after a dose is reduced, metoprolol is usually restarted straight away.

Popular FAQ

Does metoprolol cause weight gain?

Metoprolol is not likely to cause weight gain. However, weight gain can be a warning sign that heart failure is getting worse. If you are taking metoprolol for heart failure, tell your doctor right away if you gain weight suddenly. This weight gain is due to extra fluid in the body that may cause swelling in the feet, ankles, legs and belly. The fluid builds up because the heart isn’t working as well as it should.

Does metoprolol lower blood pressure?

Yes, metoprolol causes the blood vessels to relax, which helps to lower blood pressure. If you are prescribed metoprolol for high blood pressure, your doctor may ask you to check your blood pressure regularly at home with an automatic, cuff-style device that goes around your upper arm. Keeping track of your blood pressure at home is one way to help your doctor know if the prescribed dosage of metoprolol is working for you.

Should I take metoprolol in the morning or at night?

When you take metoprolol depends on which salt form is prescribed.

  • Metoprolol tartrate is usually taken twice daily, so it may be taken in the morning then at night.
  • Metoprolol succinate is taken once daily, so it may be taken either morning or night.
  • Both forms are usually taken with or immediately after a meal.

Follow your health care provider’s directions for when to take metoprolol.

What is the best time of day to take blood pressure medication?

Research has suggested that taking your blood pressure medication at night instead of in the morning can provide better blood pressure control at night without compromising daytime blood pressure control and reduce your overall risk of dying because of cardiovascular disease by 45%. Metoprolol is available as two different salts: metoprolol tartrate and metoprolol succinate.

The main difference between metoprolol tartrate and metoprolol succinate is that metoprolol tartrate is only available as an immediate-release tablet which means it must be taken several times per day, whereas metoprolol succinate is an extended-release tablet that can be taken once a day.
